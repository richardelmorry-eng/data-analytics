EXERCISE 3.B EXPLORING PUBLIC DATA

1. Start by reviewing the data.gov home page. What is this?
Answer: Data.gov is the U.S. government's official website for open data. It is a catalog where anyone can search for and download datasets published by federal agencies, for use in research, apps, and data visualization. The home page says "The Home of the U.S. Government's Open Data" and showed 584,318 datasets available. It has a search box and shortcuts to Most Viewed Datasets, Recently Added Datasets, Datasets by Organization, Geospatial, and Code Repository.

2. Go to Most Viewed Datasets. How many datasets are listed?
Answer: The Most Viewed Datasets page lists 20 datasets, ranked from #1 to #20, with a "Show more results" button to load more. The page also says 584,200 datasets are available on Data.gov in total.

3. Find the Topic Category for Human Health. Review the range of data sources available for this topic. Why do you think these were tagged with the Human Health category?
Answer: The Topic Category link was not on the current Data.gov site, so I searched for "human health" instead. It found 2,783 datasets. The results come from a mix of sources: a state agency (California health and human services program counts), the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (human health benchmarks for pesticides, screening of contaminants in public water supplies, and human biomonitoring guidance values), and the Department of Justice (a health screening study of Chicago neighborhoods). I think these were tagged Human Health because they all deal with people's health, such as exposure to chemicals in drinking water, health programs and services, and health screening. The range shows that human health data comes from many different agencies, not just health departments.

4. Go to the Food Price Outlook. Review the catalog page for this dataset. How can you navigate to additional datasets from the USDA?
Answer: On the Food Price Outlook catalog page, I can go to more USDA datasets by clicking the "Department of Agriculture" link next to "Published by Economic Research Service, Department of Agriculture" near the top of the page. That link takes me to the list of datasets from that organization. The same link also appears as the Organization on the search result. I can also use the "Find Related Datasets" section and click a tag (such as CPI, Consumer Price Index, or Food Price Inflation) to search for similar datasets. Clicking the organization link opens the Department of Agriculture page, which lists all 3,107 of its datasets and has a search box and filters.

5. Go back to the data.gov home page. This time, click on Datasets by Organization.
Answer: I went back to the home page and clicked Datasets by Organization. It opened an Organizations page with a search box.

6. Try searching for your city, county, and state. What organizations are returned?
Answer: I searched the Organizations page for Secaucus, Hudson County, New Jersey, and State of New Jersey. Each search returned 0 organizations ("No organizations found matching..."). So no organization for my city, county, or state was returned. This shows that the Organizations search only matches the exact names agencies use on Data.gov, and that a place's data may not be listed under the place's name.

7. Search for USDA, and click into the result. Within the USDA datasets, search for food composition. Notice that the top three results are all datasets concerning nutrition information. Try searching the USDA page for nutrition. How does that affect your results?
Answer: Searching the USDA page for "food composition" found 13 datasets. The top two are about food composition data: the USDA National Nutrient Database for Standard Reference, Legacy Release, and Composition of Foods Raw, Processed, Prepared (Release 28). The third result was about insects, so the results got less related after that. Searching for "nutrition" found 211 datasets, and the results were mostly about nutrition programs and studies, such as the Team Nutrition Resource Catalog, Nutrition Assistance Programs Performance Report, Nutrition Assistance Programs Keydata, and the Child Nutrition Program Operations Study. Those are about programs and policy, not the nutrient content of foods. So the search word changed my results a lot. This shows that the words a data source uses may not match the words I would think of, and I have to try different search terms to find what I'm looking for.

8. Go back to the results for food composition, and click on FoodData Central. Review the catalog page for this data source, then click on the button for Visit Page. Take some time to explore the FoodData Central website.
a) Try searching for butter, or any of your favorite foods. What kind of results do you get? What happens when you click on a result?
Answer: Searching for "butter" returned results sorted into five data types: Foundation Foods (6), SR Legacy Foods (104), Survey Foods (FNDDS) (213), Experimental Foods (0), and Branded Foods (64,242). The 6 Foundation Foods results included almond butter, salted and unsalted butter sticks, peanut butter, and sesame butter. When I clicked "Butter, stick, salted," a Food Details page opened with the data type (Foundation), food category (Dairy and Egg Products), and ID numbers. It has three tabs. "Components" shows nutrients per 100g (such as water, fat, sugars, calcium, and sodium) with the average amount, how each value was found (analytical or summed), the number of samples, and the min, max, and median. "Individual Samples" lists each sample's ID, the store location (city and state, such as Blacksburg, VA and Shawnee, KS), and the date it was acquired. "Other Information" lists food ontology and taxonomy links.
b) Go back to the home page. Find Foundation Foods, and click on Downloads. What kind of data can you find on this page? How big are these files? Where can you find more information on the data types available?
Answer: The Download FoodData Central Data page has downloadable files for each FoodData Central data type: Foundation Foods, SR Legacy, FNDDS (Survey Foods), Branded Foods, and a Full Download of All Data Types. They come in CSV (Excel-compatible) and JSON formats, with a Latest Downloads section and a Historical Downloads section that has older releases and supporting data. The file sizes vary a lot. The latest Foundation Foods release (April 2026) is small: the JSON file is 459K zipped (6.5M unzipped) and the CSV file is 3.7M zipped (32M unzipped). Other data types are much bigger: Branded Foods is 195M zipped (3.1G unzipped) for JSON, and the Full Download of All Data Types is 460M zipped (3.1G unzipped). More information on the data types is in the Documentation section at the top of the page, which has a link to the data type documentation, an API guide on SwaggerHub for the JSON format, and a Download Field Descriptions PDF that defines the terms and labels in the files. The left menu also has "Notes for Downloading."
